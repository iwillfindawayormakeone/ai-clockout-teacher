Candle care & safety

The first burn decides everything.

Wax has memory. If you blow a candle out before the melted pool reaches the edge of the jar, it will "tunnel" down the middle for the rest of its life. Give the first burn two to four hours.

  • Trim the wick to ¼ inch before every burn. A long wick makes a tall flame, black smoke, and a hot jar.
  • Burn 2–4 hours at a time. Long enough for a full melt pool, not so long that the glass overheats.
  • Stop at ½ inch of wax. Below that, the flame heats the glass base directly.
  • Keep it on a heat-safe surface, away from drafts, curtains, and anything that can catch.
  • Sink hole or rough top after the first burn? That's normal with natural waxes and doesn't affect the next burn.

The three warnings on every label

These follow the industry fire-safety labeling standard for candles (ASTM F2058), which the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission and the National Candle Association point makers to.

  • Burn within sight. Never leave a burning candle unattended.
  • Keep away from things that catch fire.
  • Keep away from children and pets.

Every Ember & Stone candle carries these warnings on the base, plus wick-trimming and burn-time instructions. If a candle ever behaves in a way that worries you (flame taller than 1½ inches, heavy soot, jar too hot to touch), put it out and tell us. We'd rather replace it than have you burn it.

Gemstone honesty guide

What "natural" actually means, and what we tell you.

The Federal Trade Commission's Jewelry Guides require sellers to disclose gemstone treatments that aren't permanent, need special care, or significantly affect value. We go further: we tell you the treatment status of every stone, every time.

Words you'll see in our listings

Natural, untreated
Mined stone, cut and polished, nothing else done to it. Most of our quartz and tourmaline.
Heated
Warmed to improve or change color. Common and stable in amethyst and citrine; most "citrine" on the market is heated amethyst. We say so if ours is.
Dyed
Color added. Common in howlite ("turquoise"), agate, and cheap "jade." Dye can fade or rub off. We don't sell dyed stones as anything but dyed.
Stabilized
Soft stone (often turquoise) impregnated with resin so it can be cut and worn. Legitimate when disclosed.
Coated
A surface film, like the rainbow on "aura quartz." Beautiful, not natural, and it can scratch off.
Lab-created
Same chemistry as the natural stone, grown in a lab. Real material, not mined. Always labeled.
Imitation
Glass, resin, or a different stone made to look like another. We don't sell imitations as gemstones, full stop.
Cultured (pearls)
Grown in a farmed mollusk. Nearly all pearls sold today are cultured. White freshwater pearls are routinely bleached and polished; we say so.

How we talk about meaning

Stones have carried meaning in many traditions for a very long time, and that's a lovely reason to wear one. What we won't do is promise that a bracelet will change your health, your money, your relationships, or your mind. Health-related claims require evidence under FTC rules, and nobody has it for a bracelet.

So you'll see language like "traditionally associated with," "a reminder of," and "for a personal ritual." An invitation, not a guarantee.

About "certificates of authenticity"

A printed certificate proves nothing for an everyday stone like rose quartz, and experienced collectors know it. What actually helps: a clear photo of the real beads, a named source, an honest treatment disclosure, and a seller who answers questions. That's what we give you instead.

Jewelry & bead care

Make a bracelet last for years, not months.

Putting it on

Roll a stretch bracelet over your hand like a sock. Pulling it open with two fingers is how elastic dies. If it's too tight to roll, it's the wrong size, and we'll swap it.

Water, sweat, and perfume

Take jewelry off for swimming, showering, workouts, and sleeping. Put it on after lotion and perfume. Chlorine dulls stones and weakens cord; perfume clouds pearls permanently.

Cleaning and storage

Wipe with a soft dry cloth. Sterling tarnishes naturally; a polishing cloth brings it back in seconds. Store pieces separately in their pouch so stones don't scratch each other.

Cord snapped? Bead loose? Every bracelet is double-strung and pull-tested, but elastic is a wear item. We restring any bracelet we made for free in the first year, and for the cost of postage after that. Repair policy.

Soap care

A bar that dries between uses lasts twice as long.

  • Use a draining dish. A bar sitting in water turns to mush by Tuesday.
  • Cut a large bar in half and keep the other half wrapped and dry.
  • Essential-oil scents soften with time. Use within a year for full scent; the soap itself stays good much longer.
  • Every ingredient is on the wrapper, listed by weight, including the sodium hydroxide used to make it (none remains in a finished, cured bar).
  • Patch-test if you have sensitive skin, and stop using it if anything irritates.
Material Monday

One material, explained plainly, every week.

These are posted in the Circle first and collected here. A few recent ones:

Wax

Why coconut-soy, not paraffin or pure soy?

Pure soy is prone to frosting and rough tops; paraffin throws scent hard but soots more easily in a small room. A coconut-soy blend burns cleanly, holds fragrance well, and stays creamy in the jar. It also costs more, which is part of why the candle does.

Stone

Rose quartz: pink because of titanium.

The soft pink comes from microscopic titanium-bearing fibers, which is why it's rarely transparent and never uniform. Very pale beads are still rose quartz. Hot-pink, perfectly even beads usually aren't.

Metal

Gold-filled vs. gold-plated.

Gold-filled has a thick layer of real gold bonded to brass, about 100 times thicker than plating. It lasts years of daily wear. Plating can fade in months. We use gold-filled for clasps and say so; we never call plated "gold."
